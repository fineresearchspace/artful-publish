Harry Markowitz
1927 — 2023

The economist who changed the question from “which security?” to “which portfolio?”

SCROLL

University of Chicago, early 1950s

Economics was changing.

Mathematics and statistics were beginning to reshape the way economists thought about markets and investment.

At the University of Chicago, Harry Markowitz studied economics alongside people such as Milton Friedman, Jacob Marschak, and Leonard Savage.

As a young economist, he became associated with the Cowles Commission for Research in Economics, an important center for mathematical economics and econometrics.

It was an unusual intellectual environment.

Economists were trying to turn economic ideas into something that could be measured, modeled, and tested.

And somewhere in this world, Markowitz was thinking about a very ordinary question.

How should an investor invest?

Markowitz would eventually become one of the most important figures in modern portfolio theory.

But the idea didn't begin with a complicated formula.

It began with a problem.

One influence on Markowitz's thinking was John Burr Williams' The Theory of Investment Value.

The Theory of
Investment Value
JOHN BURR WILLIAMS

Williams had approached the problem of valuing a stock through its future dividends.

The basic idea was:

A stock's value can be thought of as the present value of its future dividends.

VALUE OF A STOCK
↓
PRESENT VALUE
↓
FUTURE DIVIDENDS

It was a way of thinking about an individual security.

But Markowitz noticed something.

An investor doesn't usually own just one security.

They own several.

A portfolio.

And that changes the question completely.

From the stock to the portfolio

Imagine you have two stocks.

STOCK A · EXPECTED RETURN
10%
STOCK B · EXPECTED RETURN
8%

Which one would you choose?

Most people would choose A.

And why wouldn't you? 10% is better than 8%.

But there's a problem.

Return isn't everything.

Look again

Suppose these are the annual returns:

YearStock AStock B
1+20%+8%
20%+10%
3+10%+6%

The average return of A is: 10%. The average return of B is: 8%. So A still wins.

But look at the journey.

A: +20% → 0% → +10%   |   B: +8% → +10% → +6%

A's returns move around much more.

So now we have another question:

How much risk are we taking to earn that return?

This gives us two things to measure.

MEAN

Expected Return

How much do we expect to earn?

VARIANCE

Risk

How much do those returns vary?

Now we have a better way to compare investments.

But Markowitz wasn't finished. Because he asked a more interesting question.

What if you own both?

Why choose between A and B? What happens if you combine them?

Imagine you have ₹10,000. You could invest:

100% A
75% A + 25% B
50% A + 50% B
25% A + 75% B
100% B

Each combination creates a different portfolio. And each portfolio has:

An expected return. A level of risk.

Now the question has changed. We are no longer asking:

“Which stock is better?”

We are asking:

“Which portfolio is better?”

Suppose A and B both have risk. Does that mean putting them together simply gives you the average of their risks?

No.

Because A and B don't necessarily move together.

Imagine A falls while B rises. The two movements can partially offset each other.

And that means something incredibly important:

The risk of a portfolio depends not only on the risk of each security… but also on how those securities move with each other.

That relationship is captured by:

Covariance

And, more intuitively:

Correlation

Now the math makes sense

For a two-stock portfolio:

E(Rp) = wA·E(RA) + wB·E(RB)

That's the portfolio's expected return. But portfolio risk is:

σp² = wA²σA² + wB²σB² + 2wAwBσAσBρAB

The final term matters. Because it tells us how A and B move together.

And suddenly we can do something powerful.

We can calculate the risk and return of different combinations of securities.

Now imagine we don't create five portfolios.

We create hundreds. Or thousands.

Each one has: Expected return — and — Risk.

Plot every portfolio.

Some will be terrible. Some will be dominated by others. For example:

PORTFOLIO X
Return8%
Risk12%
PORTFOLIO Y
Return10%
Risk10%

Why would you choose X? Portfolio Y gives you more return and less risk.

Portfolio X is dominated.

Now look at all those portfolios. There is a boundary.

A set of portfolios where you cannot get a higher expected return without accepting more risk. That boundary is the:

The Efficient Frontier

possible portfolios efficient frontier

These are the portfolios that offer the highest expected return for each level of risk. Or: the lowest possible risk for each level of expected return.

And this is where Markowitz's contribution becomes clear.

He gave investors a framework for solving an optimization problem:

Given a level of risk, what combination of securities gives me the highest expected return?

Or: given a target return, what combination of securities gives me the lowest risk?

This became known as:

Mean-Variance Optimization

MEAN

Expected return.

VARIANCE

Risk.

COVARIANCE

How the securities move together.

Together, they allow us to think about the portfolio as a whole.

1952 · JOURNAL OF FINANCE

In 1952, Markowitz published “Portfolio Selection” in the Journal of Finance.

The contribution wasn't simply a new formula. It was a change in perspective.

BEFORE
Which security should I buy?
→
AFTER
Which portfolio should I hold?

The unit of analysis had changed.

From the security. To the portfolio.

And that became the foundation of Modern Portfolio Theory.

But the story wasn't over

Markowitz had shown us how to think about the best combinations of risky assets. But another question remained.

What happens when we introduce a risk-free asset?

Can an investor combine the optimal risky portfolio with something that has no—or effectively negligible—risk? And what happens to the efficient frontier then?
